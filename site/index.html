HALLMARK

Nearly every reference in a real bibliography is real. A citation verifier that flags even a few percent of the valid ones therefore raises more false alarms than true catches, however good it is at spotting the fabrications. Across the tools we tested, false-positive rates span an order of magnitude, and that spread decides which of them you can use.

HALLMARK is the benchmark we built to measure that: BibTeX entries labeled valid or hallucinated, each with the reason recorded. On them we score a DOI lookup, twelve frontier LLMs, tool-using agents, and our own rule-based verifier bibtex-updater.

What we found

Three ways verifiers fail

Each one is a different way a verifier ends up flagging references that are real.

Failure mode i

Letting the model call databases raises recall, and raises false alarms more

Give GPT-5.1 a five-call lookup budget and it finds more fabrications than bibtex-updater does, at about five times its false-positive rate. The cause is the harness rather than the model: the prompted model flags an entry as soon as any one database returns no match, and a real paper is routinely missing from one of them. Requiring the databases to agree that an entry is absent cuts the false-positive rate by more than an order of magnitude, at a cost in recall.

Mitigation: run bibtex-updater first and ask Sonnet 4.6 only about the entries it flags. That two-stage cascade reaches the same recall at roughly a quarter of the single-stage false-positive rate, so the inflation is avoidable.

Limitation: we did not ablate the agentic prompt, so we cannot say how much of this better prompting would fix.

Failure mode ii

At a realistic rate of fabrication, most flags are wrong

Suppose 2% of a bibliography is fabricated, roughly what venue audits find. Every verifier we tested then raises more false alarms than true catches. The best are wrong on most of their flags; the most aggressive on almost all of them.

Limitation: we compute these precisions from detection and false-positive rates measured on dev_public, under an assumed 2% rate of fabrication. We did not measure them on a corpus where 2% of the entries really are fabricated.

Failure mode iii

Most LLMs flag real papers published after their training cutoff

Show a model a real 2024–25 paper it was never trained on and it usually calls the citation fabricated. Two thirds of the LLMs we tested fail this way, some flagging most of the valid entries they see. Only the two models with the latest training cutoffs hold their false-positive rate where it was on the older corpus.

Mitigation: telling the model that its cutoff has passed helps, and how it helps differs by model. GPT-5.1 stops committing to almost anything, before or after its cutoff, so its clean post-cutoff record comes from abstaining rather than from judging better. Sonnet 4.6 abstains where it should and halves the false-positive rate on the entries it still judges. That the prompt works at all suggests the models are not blind to these papers, only overconfident about what they know.

Limitation: we report the rise as a description, not a diagnosis. It is confounded with whether a model could recall those papers at all.

What is in the corpus

Every entry is a BibTeX record labeled VALID or HALLUCINATED, with six checks recorded against it. Hallucinated entries also carry the kind of error and a difficulty tier. Eleven of the fourteen kinds of error come from documented incidents; we built the other three to stress-test verifiers. Click one for its definition and a real entry.
